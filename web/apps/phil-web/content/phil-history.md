## Phil  
Phil started as a joke.  
A Philosoraptor.  
A dinosaur whose entire purpose was to stare into the distance and ask the kind of stupid question that sometimes becomes less stupid the longer you think about it.  
In March of 2024, I called him Phil.  
At the time, Phil wasn't a digital identity, a security system, a smart account, or anything remotely close to what he is today.  
He was a memecoin on Ethereum Classic.  
I created $phil mostly because I wanted to learn.  
I had been in crypto for a couple of years by then, long enough to watch projects launch, explode, disappear, and be forgotten almost as quickly as they arrived. I was tired of constantly jumping to the next thing, tired of trusting people I didn't really trust, and increasingly interested in understanding how the systems I was using actually worked.  
So instead of buying another thing somebody else had built, I decided to build something myself.  
And that is where this became a story about me and ChatGPT building Phil together.  
I didn't come into this with a computer science degree.  
I didn't have a development team.  
I didn't have a cryptographer, Solidity engineer, security researcher, or systems architect sitting next to me.  
What I had was curiosity, stubbornness, a computer, and the ability to ask Chat essentially unlimited questions.  
So I did.  
At first, the questions were simple.  
How do I build a website?  
How does a smart contract work?  
How do I connect the two?  
What is an ABI?  
Why did this transaction fail?  
What does this error mean?  
How do I make this button actually do something?  
I would ask the questions.  
Chat would explain the concepts, help me reason through how the pieces fit together, generate examples and code for me to work from, and then I would actually try to make it work.  
Usually, something broke.  
Then I would come back with the error.  
We would figure out what happened.  
I would change it.  
Then something else would break.  
And we'd keep going.  
That process became Phil.  
  
The first version was simple.  
Get an ETC wallet.  
Get enough ETC for gas.  
Claim 10,000 $phil.  
I built a website.  
With Chat helping me understand the pieces, I learned enough about smart contracts to make the token claimable.  
I figured out donations.  
I dealt with SSL certificates.  
I built a token-lock contract.  
I fought with Ledger signatures.  
I learned about ABIs.  
I accidentally left values hardcoded into code.  
I made mistakes that became obvious only after I understood enough to recognize them.  
Chat helped me read the contracts, understand why certain interactions weren't behaving the way I expected, think through the frontend logic, and work through the errors one at a time.  
Then people actually used it.  
Something that had existed only in my head was suddenly being used by another human being.  
I remember writing:  
**"ngl pretty fkg cool to see frens use something you built, and it actually works."**  
It wasn't anything groundbreaking.  
But to me, it was.  
Because I had been saying for a while that I wanted to build something.  
Now I had.  
And almost immediately I started wondering what else Phil could become.  
That has basically been the story ever since.  
Every time we finally get something working, I learn enough to ask a better question.  
And then Phil changes again.  
  
At the end of March, the memecoin claim ended.  
Instead of moving on, I asked:  
**What if there were actual Phils?**  
I wanted there to be Phils on Ethereum Classic like there were mfers on Ethereum.  
So the token became art.  
The plan was generative artwork.  
Different bodies.  
Different eyes.  
Different clothes.  
Different backgrounds.  
Different traits layered together to create different versions of the same dinosaur.  
There was one problem.  
I had no real background in art.  
I also didn't know how to make generative art.  
So I started asking Chat.  
What kinds of algorithmic art exist?  
How could we fill SVG silhouettes?  
How do layers work?  
How could traits be randomly generated?  
How many combinations could a set of categories create?  
How could rarity work?  
How could all of this be assembled at mint?  
Those questions introduced me to things I had never heard of.  
Fractals.  
Voronoi patterns.  
Procedural generation.  
SVG path data.  
Color systems.  
Opacity.  
Stroke widths.  
Randomized geometry.  
Combinatorics.  
Chat would explain them, show me approaches, help me write and modify scripts, and help me understand what the output was actually doing.  
Then I would start playing with it.  
Changing things.  
Breaking things.  
Combining ideas.  
Rejecting things I didn't like.  
Trying something else.  
Phil started growing layers.  
Then more layers.  
Then hats.  
Then honorary Phils.  
Then hundreds of possible traits.  
And eventually I converted the artwork into SVGs.  
Which is when I learned that most of what I had just created was basically useless for the architecture I had imagined.  
The files were huge.  
So I asked Chat another question:  
**How the hell do we make this smaller?**  
  
That question pulled us into data compression.  
I learned how to gzip SVG files.  
Then I learned how to use Python to turn the compressed data into hexadecimal.  
Then we worked through how a smart contract could store that hexadecimal data.  
Then how another process could retrieve it.  
Then how to decompress it.  
Then how to reconstruct the image.  
Chat helped me understand the storage model, write and revise the scripts, reason through contract structures, interpret the bytes, and troubleshoot why the retrieved data wasn't always turning back into what I expected.  
Eventually, I got three of the eight traits to render from smart-contract data.  
To somebody who had been doing this for years, it probably wasn't particularly impressive.  
To me, it was enormous.  
The dinosaur was beginning to exist inside the blockchain.  
And somewhere in that process, another idea became important to me:  
**If Phil belonged to the user, the important parts of Phil shouldn't depend on somebody else's server staying alive.**  
That became one of the first principles that survived every version that followed.  
  
The art got more complicated.  
Hundreds of traits.  
Different generation systems.  
Chunked data.  
Base64.  
Swarm.  
Ethscriptions.  
Different approaches to rendering.  
Different approaches to storage.  
Different approaches to minting.  
I would come to Chat with some new idea at midnight and ask:  
What if we did it this way?  
Sometimes Chat would tell me it was possible.  
Sometimes it would explain why it was probably a terrible idea.  
Sometimes it would confidently suggest something that later turned out not to work the way either of us expected.  
Then reality would settle the argument.  
We would test it.  
It would work or it wouldn't.  
If it didn't, we'd figure out why.  
That distinction became increasingly important.  
Chat wasn't some magic machine producing a finished Phil for me.  
I wasn't sitting there saying, "Build Phil."  
I was learning how Phil worked by building him.  
Chat gave me a way to interact with subjects I had no formal background in.  
It could explain a concept at whatever depth I needed, help translate an idea into code, compare approaches, inspect failures, and preserve enough context that tomorrow's question could begin where yesterday's stopped.  
I brought the direction.  
The taste.  
The questions.  
The decision about what Phil should be.  
Chat helped me understand how to get there.  
And the blockchain was the thing that told both of us when we were full of shit.  
  
At one point, Phil was definitely launching July 3, 2024.  
He did not.  
I couldn't figure out how to chunk all of the artwork, store the strings on-chain and reassemble them in a way I could actually afford.  
So we started investigating other approaches.  
Swarm.  
Chunking.  
Different encoding structures.  
Different reconstruction strategies.  
I had more than 400 traits at one point.  
I kept changing the architecture.  
I remember realizing that I had spent days trying to force one process to work when there were several entirely different routes to the same destination.  
That became another thing Phil taught me.  
I can become obsessed with solving a particular implementation instead of asking whether that implementation is even the thing that matters.  
Chat became useful here too.  
Not just for answering:  
"How do I make this work?"  
But eventually for helping me ask:  
**"Should we even be doing this?"**  
That is a much better question.

Is the contract expected?

**→ SEMANTIC INTENT**  
**→ AUTHORIZATION ENVELOPE**  
The dinosaur had gone from being something held by a wallet to something capable of deciding what a wallet was allowed to do.
